Commercial energy storage procurement: what to verify before you order

Commercial energy storage is bought to solve a specific electricity problem, not to fill a datasheet. This guide sets out the load data, safety scope, performance terms and commissioning evidence a buyer should require before comparing commercial battery storage quotes.

What commercial energy storage is bought to do

Commercial and industrial battery storage sits behind the meter at a factory, warehouse, office or campus. It stores electricity when power is cheap or plentiful and discharges it when the site needs it, whether that is during expensive tariff periods, demand spikes, solar ramp-downs or grid outages.

Most buyers have one of four jobs in mind: cut demand charges, shift solar generation, protect critical loads, or defer a grid upgrade. Each job changes the power rating, usable energy, controls and interconnection. Write the job in one sentence before comparing any quotes.

Start with the load profile, not the battery datasheet

A credible proposal starts with at least twelve months of interval data, ideally at 15-minute resolution. The buyer should also provide the current tariff and contract, maximum demand, load shape, existing solar or generators, critical loads, single-line diagram, available footprint and the grid connection limit.

A system sized from an average monthly bill will usually be wrong. Demand charges depend on power over a short interval, backup depends on critical-load energy and start-up surges, and solar shifting depends on the generation profile. Better data produces a smaller, cheaper and more useful system.

  • Twelve months of interval load data, with the date, interval length and units stated.
  • The tariff structure, including demand charges, time-of-use bands and export limits.
  • A single-line diagram showing existing solar, generators, UPS systems and the point of connection.
  • Critical loads and their start-up characteristics, not just their running load.
  • Site constraints: footprint, clearance, floor loading, ambient temperature, ventilation and fire access.
  • The grid operator’s connection limit and any import or export restrictions.

Size energy and power separately

Kilowatt-hours describe how long the system can run; kilowatts describe how fast it can charge or discharge. A proposal should state the power conversion system rating, nameplate energy, usable energy, depth of discharge, round-trip efficiency, degradation assumptions and any planned augmentation.

Ask for the load-profile simulation behind the headline numbers. Confirm the guaranteed usable energy at the beginning and end of warranty, at a defined temperature and charge or discharge rate. If the supplier cannot show the assumptions, the quote is not comparable with another supplier’s quote.

Which safety standards should appear in the offer?

UL 9540 is the Standard for Energy Storage Systems and Equipment and applies to the complete system, not just the cells. UL 9540A is the test method for evaluating thermal runaway fire propagation in battery energy storage systems, and NFPA 855 is the installation standard for stationary systems [2][3][4].

IEC 62619 covers safety requirements for industrial lithium cells and batteries, including stationary applications, but it is not a complete-system certification [5]. A cell certificate is not a system certificate, and a system certificate is not an installation approval. Ask for the certificate scope, edition, issuing body, product model and the authority having jurisdiction’s acceptance path.

Performance claims: what to put in writing

Compare round-trip efficiency at a stated rate and temperature, usable energy, the degradation curve through the warranty period, auxiliary loads such as heating, ventilation and controls, availability, response time and ramp rate. A single efficiency percentage without boundary conditions is a marketing number.

A performance guarantee should name the test conditions, measurement points, allowed degradation, the remedy if performance falls short, and who pays for the test. Remedies can be augmentation, replacement or a financial credit; each has a different value to the buyer.

Warranty, service and software access

Ask who commissions the system, who services it, the response time, spare-parts arrangements, local technician coverage, firmware update policy, remote monitoring, data ownership and export, cybersecurity and end-of-life recycling. These terms decide the cost of ownership after the equipment arrives.

Software can lock an owner into a service contract. Require read-only access and data export at handover, and make sure the warranty survives if the original installer is replaced. A warranty that depends on one local company is weaker than one that follows the equipment.

Commissioning and acceptance tests

Acceptance should include a capacity test at defined conditions, protection-setting checks, point-to-point verification, emergency-stop tests, fire-alarm and suppression interface checks, SCADA or energy-management-system integration, grid-protection tests, as-built drawings, an operation and maintenance manual, and operator training.

Hold retention until the test reports and required authority approvals are delivered. A commissioning checklist in the contract is more useful than a promise that the system will be “fully tested”.

A short RFQ checklist for commercial storage

A good RFQ lets suppliers price the same scope. It should include the site data, the job the system must do, the required availability, the connection limits and the documents the buyer will use to accept the system.

  • Load data, tariff, maximum demand and critical-load list.
  • Single-line diagram, site layout, ambient conditions and fire-access constraints.
  • Required power, usable energy, backup duration and daily cycle profile.
  • Certification scope required for the target market and the authority having jurisdiction.
  • Performance guarantee, warranty, service response and software access terms.
  • Commissioning scope, acceptance tests, training, documentation and retention.
  • Delivery destination, Incoterm, transport responsibilities and target commissioning date.
  • A request for the assumptions behind the sizing, not just the price.

What a storage manufacturer’s website should publish

Buyers research before they contact a supplier. Publish the system architecture, battery and power-conversion specifications, certificate scope, fire-test summary, warranty summary, commissioning process, service coverage and an RFQ data sheet in HTML rather than only in a PDF catalogue.

Questions

Common questions

What size commercial battery storage do I need?

Sizing starts from interval load data and the bill problem, not from a standard kWh figure. Model twelve months of load against the tariff, then confirm the critical-load energy and the site’s connection limit.

Is UL 9540 the same as UL 9540A?

No. UL 9540 certifies a complete energy storage system and its equipment, while UL 9540A is the test method for evaluating thermal runaway fire propagation in battery energy storage systems.

Does IEC 62619 certify a complete energy storage system?

No. IEC 62619 covers safety requirements for industrial lithium cells and batteries; complete-system certification and installation approval are separate.

What warranty terms should a commercial BESS include?

The warranty should state usable energy and degradation limits, availability, response times, exclusions, the remedy if performance falls short, and whether it transfers to a new owner.

Can a Chinese manufacturer quote DDP for a battery system?

It can, but DDP requires the seller to clear import in the buyer’s country. Check who can act as importer of record, how dangerous-goods transport is handled, and who commissions the system before accepting DDP.

What should an RFQ for commercial storage include?

Include interval load data, the tariff, maximum demand, critical loads, the single-line diagram, site constraints, required certificates, warranty terms, commissioning scope and the delivery destination.
