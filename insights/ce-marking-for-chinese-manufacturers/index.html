CE marking for Chinese manufacturers

CE marking is the manufacturer’s own declaration that a product meets the EU harmonisation legislation that applies to it. It is not an EU approval, it is not a quality grade, and it is compulsory only for products covered by those rules. This guide explains what a Chinese manufacturer has to produce, who signs it, and when a notified body is involved.

What the CE mark means, and what it does not

The letters “CE” on a product sold in the European Economic Area signify that the product has been assessed against the EU rules on safety, health and environmental protection that apply to it. By affixing the mark, the manufacturer declares that the product meets all the legal requirements for CE marking and may be sold throughout the EEA.

The European Commission is explicit about the limits. CE marking is not an approval issued by the EU or by any authority, it does not indicate that a product has been approved as safe, and it does not indicate the product’s origin. It is a declaration of conformity made by the party that places the product on the market.

Which products need CE marking

CE marking is compulsory only for products covered by the EU harmonisation legislation that requires it, and it is forbidden on products outside that scope. A factory that adds the mark to an uncovered product is not being cautious; it is making a false declaration.

In practice the scope is set by the legislation for the product family, not by the product’s name. Electrical equipment, machinery, radio equipment, medical devices, personal protective equipment, toys, pressure equipment and construction products each sit under different rules with different assessment routes.

Who is responsible for compliance

The manufacturer carries the core duties: carry out the conformity assessment, set up the technical file, issue the EU declaration of conformity and affix the CE marking. For an exporter in China, “manufacturer” means the party that designs and produces the goods, whether or not it is established in the EU.

Importers and distributors have their own obligations. They must place only compliant products on the EEA market, keep the documentation available, and add their name and contact details where the legislation requires it. A European customer asking for your declaration is not being difficult; that customer is being asked to prove compliance by its own market surveillance authority.

The technical file and the EU declaration of conformity

The technical file is the evidence behind the mark: drawings, specifications, risk assessment, test reports, the standards applied and the assessment records. Most harmonisation legislation requires the manufacturer to keep it available for a period after the product is placed on the market, typically ten years.

The EU declaration of conformity is the signed document that lists the product, the manufacturer, the legislation applied, the harmonised standards used and, where relevant, the notified body. It is the document a buyer, a customs officer or a market surveillance authority asks for first, and it is the one document a factory should be able to send within a day.

A common gap on manufacturer websites is a wall of certificate images with no declaration and no directive numbers. Buyers who know the rules read the declaration, because it names the legislation and the standards; a certificate image without that context proves very little.

When a notified body is needed

For many products the manufacturer can complete the assessment itself, through internal production control and the applicable harmonised standards. For others the legislation requires a notified body, or gives a choice between routes, for example with higher-risk machinery or certain medical devices.

A notified body is designated by an EU country for a defined scope, and its four-digit identification number appears next to the CE mark when the legislation requires third-party involvement. A body outside that scope cannot certify the product, whatever the certificate says.

The machinery transition: directive now, regulation from 2027

Machinery is the sector where the rules are changing soonest. The current text is the Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC. The Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 was adopted on 14 June 2023 and applies on a mandatory basis from 20 January 2027.

The regulation keeps the essential health and safety requirements and voluntary harmonised standards, and adds provisions for machinery with AI-powered safety functions, cyber-safety for compliance-relevant software, and conformity assessment for higher-risk machinery. Machinery placed on the EU market before 20 January 2027 must comply with the directive.

For a Chinese machinery maker, that date belongs in the sales conversation and on the website: buyers are already asking which text a machine is declared against.

Voluntary certificates that carry no legal weight

The European Commission warns about certificates issued by bodies acting outside their notified status. Under EU law, voluntary or additional certificates are not a recognised means to prove compliance and carry no value in checks by market surveillance authorities or customs.

CE marking can only be affixed after testing and the conformity assessment procedure that the applicable legislation prescribes, and a certificate must not itself bear the CE mark. A supplier that offers a “CE certificate” as the whole answer is describing a document that does not exist in EU law: the instruments are the declaration of conformity and the technical file.

The commercial consequence is simple. A buyer who receives a voluntary certificate instead of a declaration learns that the supplier does not know the rules, and moves to the next quotation.

What to publish on the website so buyers can check

Buyers check compliance before they check price. A compliance page that names the legislation for each product family, states the assessment route, lists the harmonised standards applied and offers the declaration of conformity on request removes a real objection.

Three details make that page credible: the exact directive or regulation number, the notified body number where one is involved, and the date the declaration was signed. A fourth helps search: describe the product family in the words buyers use, such as “CE marking for industrial machinery” or “EMC and LVD compliance for control panels”, rather than a generic “certificates” heading.

Compliance content is also one of the few places where a manufacturer can answer an AI assistant’s question with a named legal instrument. That is the difference between being summarised as a supplier and being cited as a source.

Questions

Common questions

Is CE marking a quality mark?

No. CE marking declares conformity with the EU legislation that applies to the product. It is not an EU approval, not a safety certificate issued by an authority, and not an indication of origin.

Can a Chinese manufacturer self-certify?

For many product families, yes: the manufacturer completes the conformity assessment, builds the technical file and signs the EU declaration of conformity. Where the legislation requires a notified body, self-declaration is not enough.

Which directive applies to my product?

The one that covers the product family: for example 2006/42/EC for machinery until 20 January 2027, 2014/30/EU for electromagnetic compatibility, 2014/35/EU for low voltage equipment and 2014/53/EU for radio equipment. A product can fall under more than one.

What is the difference between a CE certificate and a declaration of conformity?

There is no general “CE certificate” in EU law. The binding documents are the EU declaration of conformity, signed by the manufacturer, and the technical file that supports it. Certificates from non-notified bodies do not prove compliance.

How long must the technical file be kept?

Most harmonisation legislation requires the manufacturer to keep the technical file and the declaration available for about ten years after the product is placed on the market. The exact period is set by the legislation for that product family.
