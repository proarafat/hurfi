Incoterms 2020 explained for exporters

Incoterms 2020 are the International Chamber of Commerce’s eleven three-letter rules that decide who arranges carriage, who pays which cost, and where risk passes from seller to buyer. This guide covers the rules a Chinese manufacturer is asked to quote most often — EXW, FCA, FOB, CIF, DAP, DPU and DDP — and what each one obliges the seller to do.

What Incoterms 2020 actually decide

Incoterms are eleven three-letter trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce. The current edition, Incoterms 2020, has been in force since 1 January 2020 and replaced the 2010 edition; the rules themselves have been used in international sales contracts since 1936.

An Incoterm answers three questions and nothing else: who contracts and pays for carriage, where the seller’s risk ends, and which costs each side carries. It does not set the price, the payment method, the product specification, the warranty or the law that governs the contract. A quotation that says only “FOB Ningbo” still leaves payment terms, inspection and after-sales to be agreed in writing.

The rules bind only when the contract incorporates them by reference, including the edition and a named place. “Incoterms 2020 DAP Rotterdam” is a usable term; “DAP Europe” is not.

The eleven rules fall into two groups

Seven rules apply to any mode of transport, including containers and multimodal shipments. Four apply only to sea and inland waterway carriage, and they assume the goods are handed over at the ship rather than at an inland terminal.

  • Any mode: EXW (Ex Works), FCA (Free Carrier), CPT (Carriage Paid To), CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To), DAP (Delivered at Place), DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded), DDP (Delivered Duty Paid).
  • Sea and inland waterway only: FAS (Free Alongside Ship), FOB (Free On Board), CFR (Cost and Freight), CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight).
  • The common mistake: using FOB or CIF for a container that is handed to a carrier at a container yard inland. Risk passes when the goods are on board, but the seller lost control of them days earlier.

EXW and FCA: the difference that matters to a Chinese factory

Under EXW the seller makes the goods available at its own premises and does almost nothing else: the buyer loads, clears the goods for export and arranges carriage. That looks simple for the seller, but it collides with how China exports in practice, because export declaration and VAT documentation are normally handled by the exporter.

Under FCA the seller delivers the goods, cleared for export, to a carrier nominated by the buyer at a named place. When that place is the seller’s premises, the seller loads the vehicle; when it is another place, such as a freight forwarder’s warehouse, the seller delivers the goods ready for unloading.

For most Chinese manufacturers FCA is the honest minimum term. It keeps export clearance where the paperwork and the tax refund already sit, and it stops the quotation from implying a service the factory cannot legally complete for a foreign buyer.

FOB, CFR and CIF: risk leaves early, documents carry the trade

Under FOB the seller delivers the goods on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment; risk passes at that moment. CFR moves the freight cost to the seller but not the risk: the seller pays carriage to the destination port while risk still passes when the goods are on board.

CIF adds insurance to CFR. Incoterms 2020 keeps Institute Cargo Clauses (C) as the default level of cover for CIF, which is narrower than many buyers assume, and the parties may agree a higher level. The point for a manufacturer is that the buyer’s protection comes from the document set and the insurance cover, not from a promise that the goods will arrive intact.

These three rules reward precision in the quotation: port name, vessel or sailing window where relevant, and the exact scope of what the price includes.

DAP, DPU and DDP: who clears customs and who unloads

DAP means the seller delivers the goods at the named place, ready for unloading, with import clearance and duties left to the buyer. DPU means the same journey plus unloading by the seller; it is the rule renamed from DAT in the 2020 edition.

DDP puts import clearance, duties and taxes on the seller. It is the widest obligation in the set, and the one that most often goes wrong when a factory quotes it without a customs representative or a tax registration in the destination country. A DDP price that ignores import VAT is not a price, it is a future dispute.

A practical rule: choose the smallest obligation you can actually perform. Sellers who quote DDP “to be helpful” end up owning a customs problem in a country where they have no standing.

What changed in Incoterms 2020

The 2020 edition changed presentation and a few mechanics rather than the basic allocation of risk. Five changes matter to a manufacturer that quotes every week.

  • DAT became DPU and moved after DAP, to make the seller’s unloading obligation explicit and to allow any place of destination, not only a terminal.
  • FCA now allows an on-board bill of lading. Where goods are sold FCA for sea carriage and a letter of credit requires an on-board bill, the parties may agree that the buyer instructs the carrier to issue one to the seller after loading.
  • All costs sit together at A9/B9 of each rule, so the full cost list can be read at a glance instead of being assembled from several articles.
  • Insurance levels are split. CIF keeps Institute Cargo Clauses (C) as its default; CIP now requires cover at the Institute Cargo Clauses (A) level or equivalent.
  • Security obligations are clearer in articles A4 (carriage) and A7 (export and import clearance), with the related costs listed in the consolidated costs article.

Where trade terms belong on a manufacturer’s website

Overseas buyers ask about terms before they ask for a price, and a terms block on the quotation or product page removes one email round from every negotiation. State the rule, the named place and the port, for example “FCA Ningbo, Incoterms 2020”, and keep payment terms in a separate line.

Two mistakes are common on Chinese manufacturer sites. The first is a table that presents CIF as door delivery, which leads the buyer to expect a service the term does not include. The second is a single “FOB China” line with no port, which makes freight quotes impossible to compare.

A short terms section also gives an answer engine something concrete to quote when a buyer asks what a supplier’s shipping terms are. That is worth more than another paragraph about company history.

Questions

Common questions

Are Incoterms 2020 mandatory?

No. They apply only when the sales contract incorporates them by reference, naming the edition and a place. Without that reference, the default rules of the applicable law decide delivery and risk.

What is the difference between CIF and CIP?

CIF applies only to sea and inland waterway carriage and defaults to Institute Cargo Clauses (C) cover. CIP applies to any mode of transport and requires cover at the Institute Cargo Clauses (A) level, which is broader.

Can we quote FOB for a container shipment?

It is not advisable. FOB assumes the goods are handed over on board a vessel; a container is usually handed to a carrier at an inland terminal, so risk and control separate in a way the rule does not describe.

Who pays import duty under DAP?

The buyer. Under DAP the seller delivers to the named place ready for unloading, and the buyer clears the goods for import and pays duties and taxes.

Does DDP include VAT and import taxes?

Yes. DDP places import clearance, duties and taxes on the seller, which is why a DDP price requires a customs representative or tax registration in the destination country.

Do Incoterms decide when the seller gets paid?

No. Payment terms are separate: a letter of credit, an advance payment or open account. An Incoterm only allocates carriage, cost and risk.
