How a manufacturer should choose an export marketing agency

Choosing an agency to help you sell abroad is a significant decision for a factory. A good agency will make your company easier to find and easier to trust. A poor one can waste a year and leave you with accounts you do not own. These are the questions we would ask, including of ourselves.

Start with the outcome you want

Before speaking to agencies, write down what success looks like in a year: more qualified RFQs from certain countries, a site that passes buyer checks, or less dependence on one marketplace. An agency should ask you this in the first meeting. If it starts with a package price instead, be careful.

Be wary of guarantees

Google’s own advice on hiring an SEO states plainly that no one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google. An agency that guarantees rankings or a fixed number of leads is either measuring something meaningless or promising something it cannot control.

A good agency commits to work, process and honest reporting instead.

  • Guaranteed first-page rankings
  • Guaranteed lead numbers regardless of product or price
  • Large volumes of “backlinks” for a fixed fee
  • Reports full of impressions and reach but no inquiries

Make sure you own everything

Your domain, website, hosting account, Google Search Console, analytics and ad accounts should be registered to your company, with the agency given access. If you part ways, you keep the history and the assets.

  • Domain registered in your company’s name
  • Search Console and analytics owned by your company account
  • Ad accounts in your name, with your payment method
  • Source files and content delivered to you

Ask how content gets written

For a manufacturer, content quality depends on technical accuracy. Ask who writes product pages, how they learn your products and who approves technical claims. Google’s guidance on helpful, people-first content suggests asking “Who, How, and Why” about each page; an agency should be able to answer those for the content it produces.

Mass-produced pages with little original information are a risk, not an asset. Google’s spam policies describe practices such as scaled content abuse — many pages generated mainly to manipulate rankings — and link spam.

Agree what will be reported

Ask to see a sample report. The useful numbers for an exporter are qualified inquiries by source and market, search impressions and clicks for the products you care about, and the cost per qualified inquiry for paid campaigns.

Agree a monthly report and a quarterly review where the plan can change.

Start with a defined first scope

A sensible first project is small enough to judge: fixing the main product pages, building an RFQ path, or setting up search tracking. You will learn more about an agency from three months of real work than from any proposal.

Questions to ask before you sign

A short list of direct questions tells you more than a long sales deck. Ask every agency the same questions and compare the answers in writing.

  • Who will do the work each month, and in which language do they write?
  • Which of these tasks are done by people, and which by software or AI tools?
  • What will you need from our engineers and sales team, and how much of their time?
  • Which reports will we get, and can we see an example from a similar engagement?
  • What happens to the website, domains, ad accounts and content if we stop working together?
  • How will you handle technical claims, certifications and safety statements?

Red flags in proposals

Some warning signs come up again and again. Google’s spam policies describe link schemes and scaled content abuse — producing many pages mainly to manipulate rankings — as practices that can lead to lower rankings or removal from results. If a proposal promises hundreds of backlinks or “500 AI-written pages a month”, treat that as a risk, not a bonus.

Be cautious too of proposals that report only rankings for long lists of keywords nobody searches for, or traffic without inquiries. For a manufacturer, the useful numbers are qualified inquiries, RFQs and, eventually, orders.

  • Guaranteed first-page rankings or a fixed number of leads.
  • Bulk link building or “private networks”.
  • Ownership of your domain or ad account held by the agency.
  • No access to the analytics and Search Console data.

How to compare two shortlisted agencies

When two agencies look similar, give each the same small paid task: for example, rewrite one key product page, or audit your top ten pages. Compare the quality of the work, how many questions they asked about your product, and how they handled technical details.

An agency that asks for datasheets, test reports and examples of real buyer questions is usually one that will write pages buyers trust. An agency that delivers quickly without asking anything has probably written something generic.

Questions

Common questions

Can any agency guarantee Google rankings?

No. Google states that no one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google. Treat guarantees as a warning sign.

Who should own our website and accounts?

Your company. The agency should have access, not ownership, so you keep everything if you change agency.

What should an agency report each month?

Qualified inquiries by source and market, search impressions and clicks for priority products, and spend and cost per inquiry for paid campaigns.

Is it a problem if an agency builds hundreds of pages quickly?

It can be. Pages with little original value can fall under Google’s spam policies on scaled content. Fewer, better pages are usually safer.

Should the agency understand our industry?

It should be willing to learn it and to work with your engineers. Ask how they will learn your products and who approves technical claims.

How long should the first contract be?

Long enough to do real work, short enough to judge. A defined first scope over a few months is reasonable.

Should we choose a Chinese agency or a foreign one?

Choose the one that can write naturally for your buyers and communicate well with your team. Ask to see English content they have written.

What are signs of a good agency?

They ask about your goals, admit what they cannot promise, put accounts in your name and show you real reporting.
