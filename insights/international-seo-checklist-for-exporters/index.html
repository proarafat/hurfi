An international SEO checklist for Chinese exporters

Selling abroad means being found in Google by buyers who search in their own language and their own words. This checklist covers the decisions that matter most for a Chinese manufacturer’s export website, in the order we usually work through them.

1. Decide which markets come first

International SEO starts with a business decision, not a technical one. List the countries where you already have certification, logistics and someone who can answer buyers. Those are your first markets.

For each one, note the language buyers search in and the words they use for your product. A German engineer and an American buyer may describe the same part differently, and both differ from the term used in your Chinese catalogue.

  • Markets where your products are certified or can be certified soon
  • Markets where you can ship and support customers
  • Markets where you have sales staff who can reply in the buyer’s language

2. Choose a URL structure you can maintain

Google’s guide to managing multi-regional and multilingual sites compares the main options: country-code domains (example.de), subdomains (de.example.com) and subdirectories (example.com/de/). Each has trade-offs in cost, maintenance and how strongly it signals a country.

For most manufacturers, one .com domain with language subdirectories is the simplest to run. It keeps all links and authority on one domain and is easy to host outside mainland China, which helps page speed for buyers abroad.

  • Use one domain unless you have a strong reason for country domains
  • Keep the same structure in every language (/en/products/…, /de/products/…)
  • Do not rely on automatic redirects by IP address; let buyers choose the language

3. Write each language version properly

Machine translation of a Chinese catalogue rarely works. Terms get mistranslated, specifications lose their units and the pages miss the words buyers actually search for. Write the English version first, for overseas buyers, and translate from that.

Each language version should answer the same questions: what you make, to which specification and standard, how you prove quality, and how to send an RFQ.

  • Use the buyer’s units and standard names
  • Translate the RFQ form and confirmation messages too
  • Have a native speaker or a technical reviewer check key pages

4. Connect language versions with hreflang

Hreflang annotations tell Google which page is the equivalent in another language or region. Google’s documentation on localized versions sets two rules that are often missed: each language version must list itself as well as the others, and if two pages do not point to each other, the annotations are ignored.

Use an x-default entry for the page buyers should see when no language matches.

5. Check the technical basics

  • Pages load fast from your target markets — measure from outside China
  • Every product page has a unique, descriptive title and description
  • Specifications are in HTML text, not only images or PDFs
  • An XML sitemap lists every page you want indexed
  • Old URLs redirect to their new equivalents when you restructure
  • The RFQ form works and sends to a monitored mailbox

6. Measure by market

Set up Search Console in your company’s own account. It shows impressions, clicks and the queries that brought them, and you can filter by country and page.

Track RFQs by language and market in your analytics. Rankings are a means; qualified inquiries from the markets you chose are the goal.

7. Avoid the common hreflang mistakes

Most hreflang problems on exporter sites are small and mechanical. Google’s guidelines say each language version must list itself as well as all other language versions, and that alternate URLs must be fully qualified, including https. A Spanish page that points to the English page, but not back to itself, breaks the set.

Google also describes an x-default value for pages that serve no specific language, such as a language selector or a home page that redirects. Use it when you have such a page; do not invent one.

  • Every language version lists itself and every other version, with full https URLs.
  • The language codes are valid (for example en, es, de, pt-BR), not country names.
  • Canonical tags point to the same-language page, not to the English page.
  • New language pages are added to the set on all existing versions, not just one.

8. Do not redirect buyers by IP address

It is tempting to send visitors from Germany to the German site automatically. Google’s advice on multi-regional sites is not to use IP analysis to adapt content, because location detection is unreliable and Google may not be able to crawl all variations of the site. Instead, give each version its own URL and add a visible language or region selector.

This also helps buyers. A purchasing manager in Dubai may prefer English; an engineer in Switzerland may want German or French. Let them choose, and remember their choice.

9. Plan content per market, not just translation

Translation alone rarely wins rankings in a new market. Buyers search with local terms, standards and units. A pump that is sold by flow rate in m³/h in Europe may be searched by GPM in the United States. A cable may need UL references for North America and CE or IEC references for Europe.

Before you translate, list the ten product pages that matter most in each market, and check which terms, units and certifications buyers there use. Then write — or adapt — those pages first. Smaller, better pages in a market beat a full but weak copy of the English site.

  • Local units and standards on every specification table.
  • Market-specific certifications and documents linked from the product page.
  • Contact options that suit the market: business hours, languages and response times.

Questions

Common questions

What is international SEO?

It is the work of making a website findable in search engines in more than one country or language: choosing a URL structure, writing each language version, connecting them with hreflang and measuring results by market.

Do we need a separate website for every country?

Usually not. Google documents several options; for most manufacturers, one domain with language subdirectories is the easiest to maintain.

Is machine translation good enough?

Rarely for product pages. It often gets technical terms and units wrong and misses the words buyers search for. Translate from a well-written English version and have key pages reviewed.

What happens if hreflang is wrong?

Google may ignore it. Each version must list itself and the others, and the links must go both ways.

Should we redirect visitors by IP address?

It is better to let visitors choose. Automatic redirects can stop buyers and search engines from reaching the version they need.

Does hosting in China hurt international SEO?

It can make pages slow for buyers abroad. Many exporters host the export site outside mainland China for speed.

Which language should we start with?

English, for most exporters, because it covers many markets. Add other languages where you have buyers and staff who can reply.

How do we know it is working?

Search Console shows impressions and clicks by country and page; analytics shows inquiries by market. Look at both.
