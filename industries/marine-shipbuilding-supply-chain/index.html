Shipbuilding in 2025: the official and industry figures

The figures come from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and from the China Association of the National Shipbuilding Industry (CANSI).

  • China completed 53.69 million deadweight tonnes of ships in 2025 (+11.4%), took 107.82 million dwt of new orders (−4.6%) and held 274.42 million dwt of orders at year end (+31.5%).[1][2]
  • By deadweight, these were 56.1%, 69.0% and 66.8% of the world totals; China has led all three indicators for 16 consecutive years.[1]
  • Export ships made up 89.3% of completions, 88.2% of new orders and 92.4% of the order book. Ship product exports were US$55.08 billion, up 26.7%.[2]
  • China led new orders in 16 of 18 main ship types, and its share of new green-ship orders was 69.2%.[2]
  • New offshore engineering orders were 113 units worth US$10.5 billion, 44.6% of the world total by value.[2]
  • CANSI says shipowners urgently want green and intelligent systems and “one-stop” solutions covering fuel, finance and maintenance, and urges yards to build global marketing and service networks.[2]

For equipment suppliers the order book matters more than any single year’s deliveries: ships ordered now need engines, pumps, electrical systems, coatings and interiors for years. Owners and yards abroad still need to find, qualify and trust those suppliers, and that starts online.

What overseas buyers compare in marine and shipbuilding

Marine buyers include shipyards, shipowners, ship managers and repair yards. They compare suppliers within systems like these:

Propulsion and power

Engines, propulsion components, power conversion and battery integration: type approvals, fuel options and service coverage.

Electrical and automation

Switchboards, control panels, shipboard automation and monitoring, where class approval and integration support decide.

Pumps, valves and piping

Marine pumps, valves, heat exchangers and filtration, with material certificates and approvals.

Navigation and communication

Electronics that need approval under the Marine Equipment Directive or flag-state rules for the ships they go on.

Hull, coatings and outfitting

Corrosion protection, composite parts, interiors and HVAC, compared on performance, fire safety and lead time.

Port and shipyard equipment

Cranes, material handling and port automation, sold to terminals and yards on references and uptime.

Standards, rules and documents buyers ask about

Marine equipment is regulated by international conventions, flag states and classification societies. Buyers check these first:

  • The IMO describes SOLAS as the most important international treaty on the safety of merchant ships; its main objective is minimum standards for the construction, equipment and operation of ships, with certificates as proof of compliance.[3]
  • Under SOLAS, port State control lets governments inspect foreign ships if there are clear grounds to believe the ship or its equipment does not comply.[3]
  • Equipment carried on EU-flagged ships must comply with the Marine Equipment Directive 2014/90/EU. EMSA’s MED Portal lists more than 200,000 approved marine equipment items, and its MED e-tag lets manufacturers link certified products to the portal with a Data Matrix code.[4]
  • For the website, Google’s guidance on helpful, people-first content asks whether content shows first-hand expertise, which in marine supply means real project references and engineering detail rather than generic copy.[5]

Classification-society approvals (type approvals, works approvals) are specific to products and societies. List each one with the society, certificate number, product scope and validity, and keep the list current.

Questions overseas buyers ask marine and shipbuilding suppliers

What buyers ask first, and what your website can show so the answer is already there.

Which class societies have approved this product?

A type-approval table per product with society, certificate number and expiry.

Is it MED approved for EU-flagged ships?

If so, say for which models, the notified body and the module, and link to the MED Portal entry.

Which yards and vessel types use your equipment?

References by vessel type and yard, where you are allowed to name them.

Can you support us in ports outside China?

Service network, spare-parts stock and response times by region.

Do you supply green-fuel or hybrid solutions?

State what is in service, what is in testing and what is planned, and do not blur them.

What are delivery times against a yard schedule?

Typical lead times and how you manage changes in the build programme.

A website, SEO and RFQ playbook for marine and shipbuilding manufacturers

Marine purchasing is relationship-driven, but buyers still research suppliers online before meetings and fairs. This is the order we usually work in.

  1. Structure the site by ship system and by buyer type (yard newbuild, owner retrofit, repair and service).
  2. Publish an approvals page listing every class and MED approval with scope and validity, linked from each product.
  3. Write product pages with ratings, materials, dimensions and the vessel types they suit, with datasheets in HTML and PDF.
  4. Add references by vessel type with photos and dates you are allowed to publish.
  5. Build a service-network page with ports, partners, spare-parts stock and contact details by region.
  6. Write pages on retrofit and green-ship topics only where you have products and evidence, such as hybrid power or ballast water systems.
  7. Track which product and approval pages bring inquiries from yards and owners, and expand those.

Questions

Questions from marine and shipbuilding manufacturers

We sell mainly to Chinese yards. Why do we need an English website?

Because owners, ship managers and foreign yards increasingly specify equipment themselves, and they research suppliers in English before they accept a maker on the list.

How should we show class approvals?

In a table with society, certificate number, product scope and expiry, linked from each product page and kept up to date.

Can we mention the yards and owners we supply?

Only with permission. Where names cannot be used, describe the vessel type, size and year.

Should we write about green shipping?

Only where you have products or projects to show. Claims about green technology need evidence, and buyers check.

Can you help with trade fair follow-up?

Yes. We build fair landing pages and follow-up content so contacts you meet can find the right product information afterwards.

Will you guarantee contracts with shipowners?

No. We improve how you are found and evaluated and report what happens in search and inquiries.

Is Hurfi located in Dalian or Nantong?

No. Hurfi’s offices are in Xi’an and Bogura; we work with marine suppliers in any city remotely.
